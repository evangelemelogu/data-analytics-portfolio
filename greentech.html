Where is GreenTech losing production time?

More than half of the production batches recorded downtime. I examined the underlying records to understand the interruptions behind that figure and where an operations team should begin its review.

01 / Business context

A downtime total is a starting point.

A manager looking at production delays needs to know more than how many hours were lost. Were the same interruptions happening repeatedly? Was time being lost during cleaning, while waiting for materials, or between scheduled batches? And were the biggest losses concentrated in particular products?

That was the focus of my GreenTech analysis. I brought together production batch records, downtime entries, factor descriptions and product information to investigate the pattern of disruption between March and August 2025.

My role covered SQL validation and analysis, Power BI reporting, and the interpretation of findings for an operational audience. The result is a project that connects the detailed records to specific questions about production planning, process reliability and operator support.

The decisions this work supports

  • Which recurring delays need investigation first?
  • Where is the greatest amount of time being lost?
  • How should products and operator workloads be compared?
  • What should management monitor after making changes?

02 / Analytical approach

Build the evidence before drawing conclusions.

The analysis needed to keep production batches separate from individual downtime entries. A single batch can have several recorded causes, so counting rows after joining those records would overstate the number of batches.

1. Check the source records

I used SQL to check key fields, duplicate identifiers, missing values and references between the four source tables. I also reconciled recorded minutes and distinguished zero-minute entries from events with a positive duration.

2. Reshape the downtime data

The source records store causes across Factor_1 to Factor_13. An UNPIVOT view turns these columns into event rows, making it possible to compare factors consistently.

3. Connect events to production

A master analysis view joins batches, products and factor information. A left join retains batches without downtime; distinct batch counts protect the production totals.

4. Report frequency and duration together

I used Power BI to explore factor frequency, time lost, product performance and scheduling. Reading these measures together helps distinguish frequent interruptions from less frequent but lengthy delays.

Why do the SQL event count and dashboard record count differ?

The source contains 885 populated factor entries, including 10 entries with zero minutes. The SQL event view keeps only positive durations, giving 875 events. Both counts are valid when labelled clearly: logged records include zero-minute entries; positive-duration events exclude them.

03 / Findings and interpretation

What the records brought into focus.

01 / Reach of disruption

Downtime affected 363 of 645 batches.

At 56.28%, the affected-batch rate shows that interruptions touched a substantial share of production activity during the period.

Management implication: review the recurring causes across production, rather than treating each delayed batch as an isolated incident.

02 / Process and coordination

Cleaning, scheduling and materials all deserve attention.

Cleaning / Sanitation Cycle had 86 logged entries and approximately 55 hours of downtime. Scheduling / Coordination Delay contributed about 50 hours, and Raw Material Shortage about 46 hours.

Management implication: review preparation, handovers and material availability alongside equipment reliability.

03 / Product exposure

GreenFoam Hand Soap had the highest total downtime.

Its approximately 191 hours make it a useful starting point for a deeper review. Total hours alone cannot establish whether the product is less efficient because production volume and batch requirements also matter.

Management implication: compare downtime per batch and product mix before changing production priorities.

04 / Cause classification

Most downtime duration was classified as non-operator-related.

Operator-related factors accounted for approximately 29.86% of duration; non-operator factors accounted for 70.14%.

Management implication: combine targeted coaching with reviews of materials, machinery, utilities and coordination. Operator rankings need workload context.

05 / Monthly pattern

The busiest month for interruptions was not the worst for time lost.

April recorded the highest logged frequency, while August had the greatest downtime duration. A reduction in event count would therefore not necessarily mean a reduction in time lost.

Management implication: track frequency and duration together in the same operations review.

06 / Scheduling context

Operator comparisons need a closer look at assignments.

Paul recorded the highest downtime frequency and hours, while Linda had the highest share of delayed batches. These measures describe different aspects of the workload.

Management implication: investigate batch volumes, product assignments and the underlying causes before making individual performance judgements.

Reproduce the SQL analysis
  1. Download the four CSV datasets from the repository's data folder.
  2. Import them into SQL Server. The script expects the GreenTech_Manufacturing database and the dbo tables named products, line_productivity_batches, line_downtime and downtime_factors.
  3. Check the imported column names and types, then run the script in order. It creates the event and master analysis views before querying them.
  4. Reconcile the expected results: 645 batches, 363 affected batches, 875 positive-duration events and 31,310 downtime minutes.
Open the interactive Power BI report

Download the PBIX file and open it in Power BI Desktop. Use the product, date and operator filters to investigate individual views. Refreshing the data may require updating file paths or database connections for your own environment.

Interpret the results responsibly

Operator comparisons describe recorded associations, not individual fault. Product totals should be considered alongside batch volumes and product mix. This analysis covers the supplied reporting period; proposed savings would require validated cost assumptions and evidence from implementation.
